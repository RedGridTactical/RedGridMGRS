Six Criteria for Judging Any Offline MGRS App

1. Truly offline

"Offline support" means different things to different developers. Some apps cache the last map you viewed and call it offline. Others refuse to display anything without a live connection. What you actually need is core navigation that runs in airplane mode and a map you can pre-download before you leave the trailhead.

GPS itself does not need a cell connection. The satellite receiver in your phone listens to orbiting satellites directly, so your position keeps updating even with the radios off. The question is whether the app can compute and display your grid without a network call, and whether it lets you save map tiles for your area of operations in advance.

How to test it: Turn on airplane mode (this leaves GPS active on most phones), walk outside, and confirm the app still acquires a fix and shows a live MGRS coordinate. Then try to pan to a region you have not visited and see whether the map tiles are already there or whether you hit a gray void.

2. Standards-compliant MGRS math

An MGRS coordinate is only useful if it matches the coordinate your teammates, the topographic map, and the rescue helicopter are all working from. The reference standard is DMA Technical Manual 8358.1. A critical and frequently missed detail: the conversion must truncate intermediate UTM values, not round them. Truncation is what makes a software result match military receivers such as the DAGR and PLGR, and the NGA GeoTrans reference implementation. An app that rounds can be off by a full grid square at the boundary.

You also want the precision the situation demands. A 6-digit grid resolves to 100 meters; a 10-digit grid resolves to 1 meter. Remember that an MGRS string designates the southwest corner of its square, so a coordinate like 18S UJ 23480 06473 names a 1 m square, not an abstract point.

How to test it: Pick a known landmark, read its coordinate in the app, and compare against an independent TM 8358.1 source such as NGA GeoTrans or a trusted online converter. They should agree to the last digit. Then check whether the app actually offers 10-digit output, or quietly caps you at coarser precision.

3. Privacy: does it phone home?

A navigation app knows exactly where you are. That is a sensitive thing to hand over. Many free apps monetize by sending your position to advertising or analytics servers, and most require you to create an account before you can use them. For field work, recreation, or anything where you would rather not broadcast your movements, that is the wrong trade.

The privacy-respecting design makes zero network calls for core navigation, asks for no account, and keeps your location on the device, in memory, never transmitted.

How to test it: Check whether the app forces a sign-up. Read its App Store or Google Play privacy label for "Location" and "Identifiers" collection. If you are technical, put the phone behind a network monitor and watch whether the app reaches out while you navigate. Silence is the answer you want.

4. Land-navigation toolset

A coordinate readout is the floor, not the ceiling. Real land navigation needs supporting tools: magnetic declination so your compass bearing matches the map, a pace count for dead reckoning, back-azimuth and bearing calculations, structured reports for calling in what you see, and waypoints you can save and share. Waypoint portability matters specifically: GPX and KML import and export let you plan on a desktop and carry the route into the field, or hand it off afterward.

How to test it: List the tasks you actually do, then check the app one by one. Can you set declination for your region? Is there a pace-count tool? Can you build a waypoint and export it as GPX or KML? Can you produce the report formats your team uses?

5. Battery and field reliability

An app that drains your battery in three hours is a liability on a full-day movement. Continuous GPS and a bright screen are the main costs, but background network chatter and heavy animations add up too. An app that does no networking has one less thing quietly burning power.

How to test it: Run the app for an hour with the screen on and GPS active, then check the battery usage breakdown in your phone settings. Note whether it behaves predictably and whether it recovers gracefully after the screen sleeps and wakes, rather than losing its fix.

6. Transparent pricing

Watch for two failure modes: a "free" app that is unusable until you pay, and a paid app with murky, shifting pricing. A fair model has a free tier that genuinely does useful work, and clear, stated prices for the upgrade. You should be able to read exactly what you get for free and exactly what a subscription or one-time purchase unlocks before you commit.

How to test it: Use the free tier for a real outing and ask whether it stood on its own. Then read the Pro pricing in full. If you cannot find the monthly, annual, and lifetime numbers without starting a checkout flow, that is a yellow flag.
